The Journey · My Why

Why a Mortgage Guy
Is Running for Mayor

This is not a political story. It's a human one. It starts with a father who lost his own father too young, a mother who left my dad when I was five, and twenty-six years of questions I couldn't stop asking.

The wound

My dad never had a father long enough to learn how to be one

My grandfather died of a heart attack on a golf course when my dad was twenty-two. Gone. My dad never had a man who could show him what it looked like to be present, stable, and whole. So when my dad became a father — young, diabetic, already terrified — he did the best he could with what he had. Which wasn't much.

When I was five years old, my dad came home to a note on the table. My mom had left him. She was scared. She was young. She was just trying to find love the only way she knew how. I understand that now. After custody battles, my sister and I went to live with her. But somewhere in the chaos of new husbands and new households, I felt the distance anyway. I grew up between three homes. Eight step-siblings. Two half-siblings. Parents who married seven times between them.

Every stability felt borrowed. And somewhere in all of that, I learned the lesson that would take me thirty years to unlearn: don't trust people when they say they're staying. Because they leave.

"I spent twenty years on autopilot — a hundred women, trying to fill a void I couldn't name. Not because I was bad. Because I was searching."

The search

Twenty-six years of the wrong question

I was nineteen years old in a college philosophy class when a professor asked whether love is voluntary or involuntary. I wrote that I didn't think you could choose love — that it just happens, the way a mother's instinct for her child just happens, before thought, before language, before decision.

My professor circled it and moved on. But the question never left me.

I spent the next twenty years running — building a mortgage career, chasing relationships, performing for worth I couldn't feel no matter how much I achieved. I was living on autopilot. Same habits. Same patterns. Going through motions I'd never chosen consciously.

And then I forgot my dog in the car.

It was a hundred degrees. I got a phone call, ran inside, sat down at my desk, went into my work zone. Six hours later my wife asked where the dog was. I already knew before I ran to the car. She was on the floor. Hard as a rock. Gone.

I collapsed on the ground. My whole body shaking. I've never been so broken in my life. Not because of the dog alone — but because I finally saw clearly what autopilot had done. I was so deep in routine that I didn't even know what I was doing. And assumptions kill.

"I went to church that afternoon. Not because I had faith. Because I had nothing left to hold onto except the possibility that something larger than me was still paying attention."

The awakening

I read the Bible at 45. What I found didn't match what I'd been taught.

Every morning. Four a.m. Two to three hours. Chapter by chapter, book by book — for the first time in my life, actually present. Not performing religion. Actually reading.

And I kept finding things that surprised me. Jesus spent all his time with outcasts. The Last Supper happened at Passover — a meal celebrating freedom from a system that required blood sacrifice. What if he wasn't saying "I'm paying God with my blood"? What if he was saying "I'm ending the system that says you have to pay"?

I'm not a theologian. I'm a mortgage guy. But I'd been asking the same question since I was seven years old, watching a mother animal care for her young — knowing, not believing, knowing — that automatic, involuntary love was the realest thing in the universe. And if God is love, not "has love" or "does love" but literally IS love — then why does the institution teach that love is conditional?

That question became twenty-six years of research. Thirteen books. Over a million words. A theology built on one central argument: there is only one human need — connection — and the thread running through every soul has never been cut and never can be.

The four truths at the center of everything I've written
1
You are loved.
Not because of what you've done. Not because of what you believe. Because you exist.
2
You are not alone.
The thread cannot break. Distance is not separation.
3
You are not your worst moment.
Shame is not a life sentence. The ache you feel is not evidence that something is wrong with you.
4
It is never too late.
Hope persists until the last breath. The door was never locked.
The connection

And then I looked at my city and heard the same four cries

I didn't plan for the book and the mayor project to be connected. They felt like different parts of my life. The spiritual search. The civic engagement. But the more I paid attention to Fort Lauderdale — the neighborhoods flooding every king tide, the teachers who can't afford to live in the city they serve, the small businesses being displaced, the communities fighting to hold onto their identity — the more I heard something familiar.

These weren't just policy problems. They were human wounds wearing civic clothes.

The same four truths — applied to a city of 185,000 people
Flood Infrastructure
"You are not alone."A neighborhood that floods every king tide while the city looks the other way is a neighborhood that doesn't feel seen or protected. Leadership means showing up — especially when the water rises.
Housing Affordability
"You are loved."A teacher who can't afford to live in the city she serves is a person the city has quietly decided doesn't matter. That is not love. That is a transaction. And cities built on transactions collapse.
Neighborhood Preservation
"You are not your worst moment."A neighborhood being erased by development is being told its history doesn't count. Character is worth protecting.
Environmental Resilience
"It is never too late."The decisions being made right now about sea level rise and coastal ecosystems will define this city for fifty years. It is not too late — but the window is closing.
Economic Development
"You are not alone."Small businesses being priced off the streets they built are not just losing revenue. They are losing community. An economy that only works for investors has forgotten the people inside it.
The commitment

So why am I doing this?

I'm 47 years old. I have zero political experience. I've never run for anything. I spent the first half of my life running from the wounds my parents left me and the second half trying to understand them deeply enough to turn them into something useful.

The book is part of that. The mayor project is part of that. They are the same project wearing different clothes.

My father never had a man to show him how to be present. I spent decades paying for that absence. But somewhere in the middle of all that searching — through a green card marriage that somehow became twenty-two years of real love, through holding my daughter at forty and feeling a love so automatic I couldn't stop shaking, through the morning I found my dog in the car and finally woke up — I found something I hadn't expected.

The wound doesn't have to be the end of the story. It can be the beginning of the work.

Fort Lauderdale is a city full of people carrying wounds. Residents who feel unseen. Neighborhoods that feel unprotected. Workers who feel expendable. Communities that feel erased.

I don't have all the answers. I'm not running on a platform of solutions I invented last week. I'm running on fourteen years of showing up, learning how this city actually works, earning trust the only way it can be earned — slowly, through service, in public.

Because the only thing I know for certain — after forty-seven years, seven family marriages, a hundred women, one green card deal, one lost dog, and over a million words — is this:

The thread holds.

Even in a city. Even in a broken political system. Even in a neighborhood that floods every October. Even in a classroom where a teacher drives forty-five minutes because she can't afford to live where she works.

The thread holds.

And I intend to spend the next fourteen years proving it.
