Housing Affordability

Four Apartments for Grandparents Raising Grandkids: What the City's Latest Housing Vote Tells Me

The City Commission just put more federal money behind a small affordable-housing project for seniors raising grandchildren. I think the size of it is exactly the point.

By Jim Blackburn · October 7, 2026

I read every LauderBriefs recap the City publishes after a Commission meeting, because the small votes usually tell you more about a city than the big ones. The recap from the October 6, 2026 meetings had a line that stopped me: the Commission approved an additional round of federal funding for an affordable-housing development aimed at a very specific group of residents — seniors who are raising grandchildren.

What the Commission approved

According to the City's own recap, the Commission approved an additional $105,000 in federal Community Development Block Grant funding for a project on NE 5th Terrace. The money is reported to close the remaining financing gap on a development that will create four energy-efficient, two-bedroom apartments for seniors age 62 and older who are raising grandchildren or other related children. The City says the homes will be affordable to households earning no more than 80% of the Area Median Income, and that this builds on $400,000 previously approved plus a property donation from the Community Redevelopment Agency.

Four apartments. That is the whole project. And I think the modest scale is worth sitting with rather than dismissing.

Why four units is the story, not a footnote

It is easy to look at a four-unit project and ask why it is worth a Commission vote at all. Housing affordability is one of the five issues I spend the most time studying in Fort Lauderdale, and here is what I keep coming back to: the households this project serves — an older resident on a fixed income who is suddenly parenting again — are among the easiest for a housing market to leave behind. They rarely show up in the headline numbers, and they almost never have the time or resources to show up at a town hall and advocate for themselves.

A city reveals its priorities in who it builds for when nobody is watching. Stacking a local donation, a county-level partnership, and layered federal grants to deliver four deeply targeted apartments is slow, unglamorous work. It will not move the citywide affordability numbers. But it is a signal about the kind of resident the City is willing to go out of its way to keep housed.

The questions I am still asking

Studying the city means noticing what a recap does not tell you, too. A few things I want to understand better:

  • How long is the pipeline from a funding vote like this to a family actually moving in — and what tends to slow it down?
  • How many Fort Lauderdale seniors are in this exact situation, and is four units a pilot or the ceiling of what the City can currently do?
  • Where does the rest of the stack come from when a project like this needs to scale, and what makes a developer willing to take on something this targeted?

Those are the kinds of questions I would rather chase down now, over the next several years, than pretend to have answers to. If you know this project, this corner of the housing world, or the families it is meant to serve, I would genuinely like to learn from you.
