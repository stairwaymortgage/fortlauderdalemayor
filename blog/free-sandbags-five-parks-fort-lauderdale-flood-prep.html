Flood Infrastructure

Five Parks, Two Weekends Left: Fort Lauderdale's Free Sandbag Program

The City offers pre-filled sandbags at five locations through November. Here's what I think every resident should know before time runs out.

By Jim Blackburn · October 15, 2026

Illustration

I keep a running list of the moments when a city's flood strategy becomes tangible for ordinary people — not a bond vote, not an engineering study, but something a resident can actually carry home and stack against their front door. Fort Lauderdale's monthly free sandbag program is exactly that kind of moment, and with only two distribution weekends left in the 2025 storm season cycle, I want to make sure this dispatch gets in front of as many neighbors as possible.

What the City Is Offering

According to the City's bulletin (details available at fortlauderdale.gov/stormready), the City has been distributing pre-filled sandbags on the third Saturday and Sunday of each month through November. The two remaining dates are October 17 and 18, and November 21 and 22, each running from 10:00 a.m. to 1:00 p.m. Each vehicle can receive up to five pre-filled bags, and you must be a Fort Lauderdale resident with proof of residency. Supplies are distributed while they last, so arriving early matters.

The five distribution locations the City has designated are:

  • Floyd Hull / Morton Center — 2800 SW 8th Ave.
  • George English Park — 1101 Bayview Dr.
  • Holiday Park — 1150 G. Harold Martin Dr.
  • Mills Pond Park — 2201 NW 9th Ave.
  • Riverland Park — 950 SW 27th Ave.

For residents who need more than five bags or who missed a distribution weekend, the City also maintains daily self-fill stations at Floyd Hull / Morton Center and Mills Pond Park, open every day from 6:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m. You bring your own bags and shovel; the sand is provided. That's a genuinely useful option that doesn't get enough attention.

Why This Matters Beyond the Bags

I've been tracking flood infrastructure as one of the five core civic issues I study as part of this long-term project. Sandbag programs are often dismissed as low-tech stopgaps, and technically that's accurate — no sandbag replaces a properly designed stormwater system or a seawall built to current sea-level projections. But I think that framing misses something important about how flood resilience actually works at the neighborhood level.

The households most exposed to nuisance flooding — particularly in lower-elevation corridors in the northwest and southwest — are often the same households with the least margin to absorb property damage. A free sandbag program with five geographically distributed sites, daily self-fill access, and weekend staffed events is a genuine equity measure, not just a logistics convenience. The question I keep asking myself as I document this city is: are we building infrastructure that serves the residents who need it most? Programs like this one at least gesture in that direction.

I'd also note the geographic spread of the five parks. Bayview Drive (George English Park) serves the eastern, waterfront-adjacent neighborhoods. Riverland Park reaches the southwest. Mills Pond serves the northwest corridor. The City appears to have made a deliberate effort to distribute access across the city rather than clustering sites in one quadrant. That's worth acknowledging.

What I'd Want to See Next

From a civic-observation standpoint, a few things would make this program even more effective over time. First, proactive outreach in the weeks before distribution weekends — not just a bulletin the week of. Second, translation of distribution details into Haitian Creole and Spanish for Fort Lauderdale's linguistically diverse neighborhoods. Third, some form of after-action reporting: how many bags were distributed, at which sites, and did demand exceed supply? That data would help the City right-size future programs and help observers like me understand where flood anxiety is highest.

I've written about related flood-preparedness questions in past dispatches, and I plan to keep following how the City's stormwater and resilience investments evolve heading toward the end of the decade. If you have observations from your own neighborhood — standing water patterns, drainage concerns, or experiences at these sandbag sites — I'd genuinely like to hear them. This fourteen-year civic project is built on exactly that kind of ground-level input.

In the meantime: if you're a Fort Lauderdale resident and you haven't picked up sandbags yet this season, October 17 and 18 are your next opportunity. Bring your ID, arrive before the 1:00 p.m. close, and don't wait until a storm is already named.
