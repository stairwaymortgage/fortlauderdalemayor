Flood Infrastructure

Two Neighborhoods, $177M, and a City Choosing to Act on Flooding

The Oct. 20 Commission approved $177M+ in stormwater contracts for Riverland and Melrose Manors — here's what I'm watching.

By Jim Blackburn · October 23, 2026

Illustration

Last Tuesday night I watched the October 20 Commission meeting replay and found myself doing the math twice, because the numbers didn't feel real: the City approved more than $177 million in stormwater contracts in a single meeting. For a resident who has spent years tracking Fort Lauderdale's flood vulnerability as part of this long-term civic project, that kind of investment demands close reading — not cheerleading, not cynicism, just honest attention.

Riverland and Melrose Manors: What Was Actually Approved

According to the City's LauderBriefs recap (viewable via the City's FLTV stream at fortlauderdale.gov/fltv), the Commission approved two separate stormwater agreements on October 20, 2026. The first, valued at approximately $82.6 million, goes to David Mancini & Sons, Inc. for drainage improvements in the Riverland neighborhood — a community that currently lacks a comprehensive drainage system and floods during moderate to heavy rain. The second, roughly $94.8 million, pairs the City with Man-Con, Incorporated to build out underground drainage infrastructure and new pump stations in Melrose Manors, a neighborhood the City itself acknowledges has faced longstanding flooding challenges. Both contracts cover design and construction, with substantial completion targeted about two years after each project's second notice to proceed. Both are positioned under the City's Fortify Lauderdale initiative.

Two years is a long time to wait if your living room floods every August. But building comprehensive drainage systems — new pipes, pump stations, road restoration — isn't a weekend project. I'll be watching whether those two-year timelines hold and whether residents in both neighborhoods are kept informed as work proceeds.

The Legislative Ask: $16.3 Million and What It Signals

Alongside the flood contracts, the Commission also reviewed the City's proposed 2027 State Legislative Program — a document that lays out Fort Lauderdale's funding requests heading into Florida's next legislative session. The total ask is approximately $16.3 million across nine projects. The single largest request, $9 million, would support water and wastewater system improvements. Another roughly $5.2 million would go toward transportation infrastructure, including safety upgrades along Las Olas Boulevard and new sidewalks. The program also addresses housing affordability, local authority, public safety, and services for people experiencing homelessness — issues I track closely under the five core issues guiding this project.

State legislative programs are often treated as background noise, but I think they deserve scrutiny. What Fort Lauderdale asks Tallahassee for tells you something about what it can't fund on its own. A $9 million water-infrastructure request, sitting alongside $177 million in locally approved stormwater contracts, suggests the City is pushing on multiple fronts simultaneously — which is the right instinct, but also a coordination challenge worth monitoring.

Himmarshee and Sunrise: Two Smaller Items That Deserve Airtime

Two other items from the same meeting caught my eye. First, the Commission discussed a roughly $960,000–$985,000 proposal to enlarge the athletic field under construction at Sunrise Middle School after community members flagged that the original design was too small for full-size youth soccer. The expanded field — a 100-by-50-yard natural grass layout — would push completion to summer 2027. This is a modest number compared to the stormwater contracts, but it's exactly the kind of neighborhood-level responsiveness that builds or erodes trust over time.

Second, the Commission reviewed a 90-day tactical urbanism pilot for Himmarshee Street in the historic district downtown. The plan would convert three metered parking spaces near SW 2nd Street and SW 3rd Avenue into a temporary outdoor seating area with landscaping and possibly decorative lighting, tentatively starting Spring 2027. The Commission also approved first reading of an ordinance to retire a 1999 Special Entertainment Overlay designation that has apparently fallen out of compliance due to changes in ownership and business operations. I'll be watching the Himmarshee thread carefully — zoning overlays that outlive their original conditions are exactly the kind of structural detail that shapes a neighborhood's future without much public fanfare.

My Takeaway from One Busy Tuesday Night

A $177 million commitment to stormwater infrastructure in two neighborhoods is genuinely significant. If these projects are executed on schedule and on budget, Riverland and Melrose Manors will be materially different places to live by 2029. That's the promise. The accountability question — who tracks the milestones, who reports back to residents, what happens when timelines slip — is the part that rarely gets as much attention as the ribbon-cutting. I'll be checking back on both projects in future dispatches. If you've been following along, you can find earlier flood-related observations in the dispatch archive.
