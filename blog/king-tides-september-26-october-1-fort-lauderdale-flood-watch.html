Flood Infrastructure

King Tides Sept. 26–Oct. 1: What Every Fort Lauderdale Resident Should Know

King tides arrive this week with twice-daily peak windows. Here's how I'm tracking the event and what it tells us about long-term flood infrastructure needs.

By Jim Blackburn · October 16, 2026

Illustration

I keep a running calendar of king tide windows the same way some people track hurricane season. Not out of anxiety, but because these recurring tidal surges are, in my view, the single most honest stress-test our street infrastructure faces all year. This week's cycle — running from Saturday, September 26 through Thursday, October 1 — is a good moment to pay attention, take notes, and think about what the pattern is telling us over time.

What the City Is Telling Us

According to the City of Fort Lauderdale bulletin (source: fortlauderdale.gov/fixitftl), king tides are forecast to produce elevated water levels across six consecutive days, with peak tide windows occurring twice daily for most of the period. The specific peak times the City has published are:

  • Saturday, Sept. 26: 8:30 AM and 8:45 PM
  • Sunday, Sept. 27: 9:15 AM and 9:30 PM
  • Monday, Sept. 28: 10:00 AM and 10:00 PM
  • Tuesday, Sept. 29: 10:45 AM and 10:45 PM
  • Wednesday, Sept. 30: 11:30 AM and 11:30 PM
  • Thursday, Oct. 1: 12:30 PM

The City also notes that any rainfall coinciding with these peak windows will compound surface ponding significantly. Residents are urged not to drive through standing water, and impassable road conditions can be reported by calling 954-828-8000, emailing cservice@fortlauderdale.gov, or submitting a report through the FixIt FTL app.

Why I Watch These Windows So Carefully

King tides are not anomalies — they are predictable, calendar-driven events tied to the alignment of the sun and moon. What makes them instructive is precisely that predictability. If a street floods during a king tide, we already knew the water was coming. That means any flooding that occurs is a infrastructure readiness problem, not a weather surprise. Over the years I've been documenting this city as part of my long-term civic project, I've noticed that the same intersections and corridors tend to pond during nearly every king tide cycle. That pattern deserves systematic attention, not just a reactive heads-up bulletin.

The overlap with potential rainfall this time of year adds an important variable. Late September still falls within the heart of South Florida's wet season, so the combination of astronomical tide peaks and afternoon thunderstorm runoff is a realistic scenario — and one our drainage systems have historically struggled to handle simultaneously.

Flood Infrastructure as a Core Issue

On this site, flood infrastructure is one of the five issues I track most closely, and king tide events are a primary lens through which I evaluate the city's progress. Fort Lauderdale has made real investments in pump stations and outfall upgrades in recent years, but the question I keep asking is: are those investments keeping pace with the rate of sea-level change, and are they reaching the neighborhoods that flood first and drain last?

This week I'll be logging observations across several parts of the city during peak windows. I'm particularly interested in whether areas that have received recent infrastructure upgrades perform measurably better than they did in prior years' cycles. That kind of longitudinal, neighborhood-by-neighborhood documentation is exactly the work I think a future city leader should be doing right now — not waiting until they're in office to start learning the geography of vulnerability.

What You Can Do This Week

  • Check the City's published peak times above and plan around them, especially if your commute takes you through low-lying corridors near the Intracoastal or coastal neighborhoods.
  • If you see a road that is impassable, report it. The FixIt FTL app at fortlauderdale.gov/fixitftl creates a documented record — that data matters for infrastructure planning.
  • Take photos. Your street-level observations are part of the civic record, and they add texture that official reports often miss.
  • Note whether flooding occurs at peak tide even without rain. That baseline tells us a lot about ground-level exposure independent of storm events.

I'll be sharing what I observe in upcoming posts on the dispatch archive. If you're documenting your own neighborhood this week, I'd genuinely like to hear about it through the contact page.

Frequently asked questions

What causes king tides and why do they matter for Fort Lauderdale specifically?
King tides occur when the Earth, moon, and sun align in a way that produces the highest gravitational pull on ocean water. Fort Lauderdale's low elevation and proximity to the Intracoastal Waterway and Atlantic coast make it especially susceptible to tidal flooding during these windows, even without any rainfall.
